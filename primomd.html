01 · Problem

A week of visits, dozens of moving parts.

Post-acute care depends on the right clinical liaison seeing the right patient at the right time. Behind that sits a weekly puzzle of facilities, patients, liaisons and providers.

Care coordinators had to plan the week, spot gaps and react when things changed: a liaison on leave, two visits clashing, a patient whose risk suddenly went up. Liaisons, meanwhile, needed a simple answer to “who do I see today, and who first?”. And because the product holds patient information, access had to be secure, without making every sign-in a chore.

01

No single picture

Workload across facilities, and what’s pending, overdue or unassigned, wasn’t visible in one place.

02

Change is constant

Leave and scheduling conflicts meant assignments had to move quickly and safely.

03

High stakes, little time

Liaisons needed to spot high-risk patients instantly, and trust that the data was fresh.

How might we

…help coordinators plan and adjust a whole week of care at a glance, while giving each liaison a calm, risk-first list of who to see?

02 · Research

The central question: how might we help coordinators manage complex schedules while helping liaisons prioritise visits, see clinical context and finish their work with confidence?

03 · Exploration

Three ways to look at the same week.

Instead of one “perfect” schedule, we explored how each role naturally slices the week, and designed a view for each.

We weighed a pure calendar (great for time, poor for workload), a pure table (great for scanning, poor for time) and a hybrid. The answer was to let coordinators switch lenses: a calendar for time-based planning, and tables for facility and liaison workload.

Option A

Calendar only

Clear for time slots and clashes, but hard to compare workload across 40 facilities.

Not enough on its own
Option B

Table only

Fast to scan and filter, but loses the shape of the day and makes conflicts hard to see.

Not enough on its own
Option C

Switchable views

Liaison, provider and facility views, plus a calendar for scheduling. Each role gets the lens that fits the task.

Chosen

Filtering, explored

With up to 40 facilities and hundreds of patients, filtering had to be powerful but forgiving. We explored a single search, simple dropdowns, and a filter modal; we landed on quick filters for common cases, a modal for advanced ones, and chips so applied filters are always visible.

04 · The key trade-off

Everything at once, or calm focus?

Coordinators wanted

Density

Every facility, every status, every liaison, visible without clicking.

vs
Liaisons wanted

Focus

A short, prioritised list of patients, with nothing extra in the way.

Our call: design for the role, not the average.

Rather than one compromise screen, the product splits by role. Coordinators get dense, switchable planning views with KPI summaries; liaisons get “My assignments”, a focused list sorted around risk.

The cost: two experiences to design and maintain. We managed that with one shared design system (tables, chips, filters, status colours), so both feel like the same product while doing different jobs.

Second trade-off

Security

Patient data needs strong authentication.

vs
…against

Speed

Busy clinical staff sign in often.

We made MFA mandatory but flexible: three methods, a recommended default (authenticator app), the option to pick a preferred method, and a “try another way” escape hatch.

06 · Design decisions

The choices that shaped it.

01

Risk is visible in the list, not hidden in a record

High, medium and low with the percentage, colour-coded, so liaisons can prioritise without opening anything.

Why: the first decision of a liaison’s day is “who first?”, so that answer belongs on the first screen.

02

KPIs only shout when they need to

Pending and overdue cards change state when they’re above zero, and stay quiet otherwise.

Why: if everything is highlighted, nothing is. Alert colour is saved for real problems.

03

Always show how fresh the data is

An “Updated” timestamp with a refresh button sits on every planning view.

Why: coordinators act on the schedule in real time; they need to trust it before they change it.

04

Applied filters become chips

Every active filter shows as a removable chip, with show more / show less and reset.

Why: hidden filters are the main reason people think data is missing.

05

Explain conflicts before changing anything

Reassigning for leave or clashes shows the conflict first, then the fix.

Why: moving patient visits is high stakes; no silent changes.

06

Design every state, not just the happy path

Expired links, wrong codes, too many attempts, no results, empty states: all designed.

Why: in clinical tools, the edge cases are everyday cases.

07 · Validation

Checking it with the people who’d use it.

We reviewed the designs screen by screen with the client team, walking through key workflows and edge cases to check usability, scheduling efficiency and clarity.

The reviews focused on how coordinators assign and manage visits, how liaisons spot priority patients, and how both roles respond to schedule changes. Feedback pointed to ways to improve navigation, cut unnecessary steps and make critical information easier to reach.

09 · Reflection

What I’d tell my past self.

Designing a care coordination platform reinforced that good UX isn’t just simpler screens. It’s understanding how different roles make decisions, carry responsibilities and depend on accurate information through their day.

01

Design for the role, not the average user.

Coordinators and liaisons work with the same information but very different priorities. Designing around each workflow made both experiences clearer than one shared interface could.

02

Edge cases deserve as much attention as primary flows.

Scheduling conflicts, overdue visits, expired links and sign-in errors shape usability and trust. Mapping them early made the patterns more complete and resilient.

03

Design decisions need measurable outcomes.

Polished screens are only part of the job. Success metrics like scheduling efficiency, task completion and fewer errors connect design to real operational gains.

If I did it again

I’d bring usability testing and success metrics in earlier, to validate assumptions sooner, prioritise by real behaviour, and tie design decisions to measurable outcomes.
