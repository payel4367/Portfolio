A conversational app built around Vaani, an AI Vedic astrologer: fast onboarding, ask by text or voice, answers with references, compatibility, daily horoscopes and in-depth reports. On the web, the same guide sits beside Astropedia, calculators, blogs and reports.

My role

A freelance project: UX and product design across the mobile app and web: onboarding, questions, compatibility, horoscopes, reports, plans and profile, plus a UX audit of the shipped flows.

01 · Problem

Big questions, and nobody to ask.

“Should I switch jobs?” “Will I get married this year?” “How will my dad’s health be?” People turn to Vedic astrology at moments like these, and they want an answer about their chart, not a generic sun-sign blurb.

Booking a human astrologer takes time and money. Most apps either ask for a long form before giving anything back, or answer with content that feels the same for everyone. And because the questions are personal, people need to feel the product is private and credible before they share birth details.

01

Setup feels like work

Phone, OTP, name, date, time and place of birth: a lot to ask before someone has seen any value.

02

The blank-box problem

Many people know they’re worried but don’t know how to phrase the question, so an empty input stalls them.

03

Trust before payment

Answers need to feel personal and explainable, and offers need to feel fair, or people leave.

How might we

…get people from “I have a question” to a personal, credible answer in minutes, and give them reasons to come back, without making payment the first thing they feel?

02 · Research

Auditing every screen, honestly.

I ran a heuristic UX audit of the shipped app, flow by flow: onboarding, compatibility, profile, family members, referral and payment.

The goal: find the usability barriers and opportunities in onboarding, personalisation, navigation, trust and the move from free exploration to paid services. Every screen was reviewed against four labels: Good (keep it), Needs improvement, Needs to be fixed, and Idea, with a proposed UI improvement next to it.

03 · Exploration

Many ways in, one way forward.

Onboarding got the most exploration, because it decides whether someone ever asks a question.

The design file holds three login and sign-up variants, a set of A/B test screens, a full round of new onboarding screens that were rejected, and a revised onboarding flow. Home went through its own rounds too: separate home and questions screens were merged so the ask bar became the first thing you see.

Option A

Long form up front

Collect everything on one screen. Fast to build, but it feels like paperwork before any value.

Too heavy
Option B

Skip details, ask later

Let people in first. But without a birth chart, the first answer can’t be personal.

Weak first answer
Option C

Short steps, then a reward

Phone and OTP, name, then birth details with a fallback for unknown times, followed by a personal snapshot and a free first question.

Chosen

Home, explored

Navigation options, banner variations and a merged “questions + home” screen were tried. The winner puts the question bar at the top, with the family switcher above it and daily horoscopes just below, so asking is always one tap away.

The web, rethought

A bigger screen isn’t just a stretched phone. On the web, a persistent sidebar holds every section and your past readings, the centre stays a calm, chat-first space, and sign-in waits until a question actually needs your chart.

04 · The key trade-off

Give value first, or ask for payment early?

The business needed

Revenue

Questions, reports and compatibility checks are what people pay for.

vs
Users needed

Trust

Proof that the answers are personal before they spend money.

Our call: earn the first payment.

Every new user gets a personal snapshot and a free first question. Balances (“1 free question left”) stay visible, and offers appear at natural moments, like when the balance reaches zero.

The cost: some revenue moves later in the journey. In return, the paywall arrives after the user has felt the value, with clear plans, the discount shown upfront and a secure payment summary.

Second trade-off

Accuracy

Exact birth time gives the best reading.

vs
…against

Completion

Many people don’t know their exact birth time.

Instead of blocking, the form lets people mark the time as approximate or say they don’t know it, explains that accuracy improves with a precise time, and lets them update it later in settings.

06 · Design decisions

The choices that shaped it.

01

Suggested questions instead of a blank box

Vaani offers ready-made prompts, including trending ones and Hindi options, with a refresh to see more.

Why: many people know what worries them but not how to phrase it.

02

Ask by voice as well as text

A microphone sits in the ask bar on every screen, with a clear “Stop” state while listening.

Why: personal questions are often easier to say than to type, especially in your own language.

03

Answers show their working

“Check astrological references” opens the chart factors behind each answer, with feedback, listen and share actions.

Why: explainable answers feel credible, not generic.

04

Always know whose chart you’re asking about

A family switcher sits at the top, with a clear “Asking for yourself / Asking for family” banner.

Why: people ask for parents, partners and children, and mixing them up breaks trust.

05

Unknown birth time isn’t a dead end

Approximate and “I don’t know” options, with a note that accuracy improves with a precise time.

Why: this is the highest drop-off step; blocking it loses people.

06

On the web, ask first and sign in later

Anyone can type a question. The sign-in modal appears only when an answer needs a chart, and explains why: “This needs your birth chart.”

Why: asking is the hook; a login wall before it would lose curious first-time visitors.

07

Every tool hands off to Vaani

Calculators end with “Want it interpreted? Ask Vaani”, reports offer “Ask Vaani first”, and blogs close with “Every article, run against your own chart”.

Why: generic content becomes personal the moment it connects to your chart.

08

Balances and offers are always clear

Remaining questions, reports and compatibility checks show where they’re used, with plans, discounts and codes upfront.

Why: surprises at checkout feel like tricks; clarity feels fair.

07 · Validation

Testing what makes people stay.

The redesign set out to validate whether a more intuitive onboarding experience, clearer personalized predictions and simplified purchase flows could improve usability, build trust and encourage continued engagement.

The design exploration includes A/B variants for onboarding and the purchase and promotional modules, alongside an engagement journey for users’ first hours after signing up.

The proposed validation combines heuristic evaluation, usability testing and A/B testing to assess how effectively users complete onboarding, understand personalized insights, navigate family profiles and discover premium features.

10 · Reflection

What I’d tell myself next time.

Reward effort quickly. The moment after birth details is the one that decides whether people stay, so it deserves the most care.

Copy is UI. Small words (“Verify” on a step that isn’t verification, “Get OTP” instead of “Continue securely”) change how safe a flow feels.

Audit what you ship. Looking back at live flows with fresh eyes found issues no review of static mock-ups would.

Next time, I’d set up funnel metrics before launch, so each onboarding change could be measured, not just argued.
